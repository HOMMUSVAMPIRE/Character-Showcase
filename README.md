# Character Showcase
This Unity project is intended to showcase my capabilities in the area of creating satisfying character controllers which feel responsive, satisfying, and intuitive. It is also meant to showcase my coding style for possible employers and demonstrate expertise with C# and Unity. Peruse at your own discretion.

This project is still a work in progress. I will be adding to it in my free time as I search for new employment.

Once the project meets my standards for completion, I may release it as a Unity Asset Store package for others to use in their games.

1. Clone the repo locally (more details here if needed: https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/creating-and-managing-repositories/cloning-a-repository)
2. Download UnityHub from https://unity.com/download

## Walking and Sprinting
The character moves at its walk speed (3 m/s), or slower with partial stick input, and sprints at its run speed (5.8 m/s) while Sprint is held: Left Shift (keyboard) or pressing the left stick (gamepad). Sprinting works in the air too: steering toward the run speed, and keeping a sprint's momentum through a jump without stacking more on top. Both speeds are in the Speed section of the BasicMovementMode asset; a run speed at or below the walk speed turns sprinting off.

At its walk speed the character walks briskly: Quaternius's walk is made for about 1 m/s, so between it and their jog (5.8 m/s, what sprinting shows) the mannequin's own brisk walk (2.2 m/s) and run (3.8 m/s) fill in. At 3 m/s the brisk walk is played a little faster than it's made for, so its planted feet slide by about an eighth of the speed: a trade for a walk, not a run, at the default speed (people break into a run at about 2 m/s). Sprinting plays Quaternius's jog at its own pace, with its feet planted (see Character Model and Animations).

## Dash
Press F (keyboard) or right trigger (gamepad) to dash: a short burst in the movement direction relative to the camera, or straight ahead without input. A dash moves in a straight line, ignoring gravity and steering, and hands over to momentum when it ends instead of stopping dead. It has a cooldown, a short input buffer (pressing just before the cooldown ends still dashes), one dash per button press, and a limited number of air dashes that reset on landing. Jumping during a dash cancels it straight into the jump, keeping part of the dash's speed. Dashing during a jump cancels the jump's upward momentum, and the jump doesn't resume afterwards. The character plays a dash animation: it slides low in the dash direction with its legs trailing behind. All of it is tunable in the Dashing section of the BasicMovementMode asset, including a speed curve over the dash and how much speed is kept afterwards. A dash duration of 0 turns dashing off.

## Crouching, Sliding and Rolling
Press Crouch (C, or the east face button on a gamepad) to crouch, and again to stand: the character's collider lowers to the crouch height (55% of its standing height) and it moves at its crouch speed (2 m/s). It stands only where there's room: under something low it stays crouched, and stands by itself as soon as there's room again (a second press while it waits cancels that). Jumping from a crouch stands up and jumps, where there's room. Hold To Crouch makes Crouch a held button instead.

Pressing Crouch while sprinting (moving at least 4.5 m/s) slides: the character keeps the sprint's momentum (at least the slide start speed, 10 m/s), low enough to pass under things it can't stand under, slows down (8 m/s²), and can be steered a little. The slide ends in a crouch once it slows to 3 m/s, after 1.2 seconds, or against a wall. Pressing Crouch again stands up out of it; jumping jumps out of it keeping its speed; sliding off an edge carries its momentum into the air.

Press Roll (Left Ctrl, or the left bumper) on the ground to roll: a quick, low, committed roll in the movement direction (or straight ahead), 8 m/s easing to 60% over 0.55 seconds, which can't be steered, jumped out of or dashed out of. A roll has a short cooldown and input buffer, and one roll per press. It ends standing, or crouched where there's no room. Rolling with movement input held hands straight over to walking or sprinting as the roll ends: the character moves on at its walking speed (keeping only whatever speed the roll has beyond that), and the animation blends from the roll into the stride over the roll's last quarter, rather than stopping and starting again.

All of it is tunable in the Crouching, Sliding and Rolling sections of the BasicMovementMode asset. A slide or roll length of 0 turns it off.

## Wall-Running
Moving fast (at least 4 m/s) along a wall beside the character in mid-air, at least 0.75 m above the ground, while steering along it, runs along the wall: at the wall-run speed (9 m/s), falling slowly (5 m/s² instead of gravity; a rising jump is cut down to 4 m/s, and a fall caught), facing along the wall. The run ends after 1.2 seconds, when the wall ends, when something slows it, when steering stops pointing along the wall or points away from it, on pressing Crouch, or on landing. Jumping jumps off the wall, away from it (7 m/s) and upward (10 m/s), keeping the run's speed and giving back the character's air jumps; steering then ramps back in as after any jump, so the jump carries the character off the wall. The same wall can't be run along twice without touching the ground, but another can, after a short cooldown, so wall jumps chain between facing walls. Everything is in the Wall Running section of the BasicMovementMode asset.

## Wall Sliding
Falling into a wall head-on (within 45° of facing it, steering into it or moving toward it, at least 0.5 m above the ground) slides slowly down it: the character is pressed against the wall facing it, and its fall is braked (30 m/s²) to the slide speed, 2.5 m/s. Rising into a wall carries on rising; the slide starts once it falls. Jumping kicks off the wall, upward (12 m/s) and a little away from it (5 m/s), to gain height, and gives back the character's air jumps, as a wall run's jump does; steering back into the wall slides down it again, so a wall can be climbed in hops, up to three jumps before landing. Steering away lets go; landing, the wall ending, Crouch, or 3 seconds of sliding end the slide, and after dropping off with Crouch or sliding off the end of its time, that wall can't be slid down again before landing. Glancing along a wall instead wall-runs. The Wall Sliding section of the BasicMovementMode asset tunes it; a longest time of 0 turns it off. The mannequin's own wall-slide pose plays (hands spread on the wall at head height, one knee raised against it), and WallSlideIK puts the body against the wall and the hands flat on it.

## Ledges
In mid-air, heading toward a wall whose top is within reach of the character's hands (between 0.7 and 1.3 times its height above its feet) with room to hang below it, the character catches hold of the ledge and snaps into hanging from it: straight below the point of the edge it grabbed, its feet 1.1 times its height below the ledge, against the wall, facing it, without gravity. LedgeHandIK, on every model, places its hands directly on the ledge: the wrists on its top just back from the edge, about 0.8 m apart along it, palms down with the fingers over the edge, and moves its body flush against the wall and to the height that lets the hands reach (the collider stays its radius from the wall; the model doesn't). It eases in on catching hold, and the hands let go partway up a climb. A jump passes ledges on the way up and catches them near its top or falling (they're only grabbed rising slower than 2 m/s). Hanging, Jump climbs up onto the ledge (rising up the wall, then forward onto it, over 0.7 seconds), crouched if there's only room to crouch on top, and not at all if there isn't room for that. Where it stands on top is found by lowering it onto whatever is there, so a ledge at the foot of rising stairs climbs up onto the stairs. Crouch, or steering away from the wall, lets go, and the same ledge can't be caught again for a moment. Ledges are kept in the space of what they belong to, so the character hangs from and climbs ledges that move. The Ledges section of the BasicMovementMode asset tunes them; a climb duration of 0 turns ledges off.

## Pushing and Pulling
Large objects with a PushableObject component (a kinematic Rigidbody with a BoxCollider) can be pushed and pulled. Holding Interact (E, or the north face button) facing one of an object's sides, close to it, takes hold of it: steering toward it then pushes it, and away from it pulls it, at 1.8 m/s times the object's own Speed Multiplier (lower for heavier objects); sideways steering does nothing. Objects can be made push-only (Can Be Pulled). An object's moves are swept against the world, so it stops at walls, and the character stops with it. Pushed off an edge, an object falls (it has simple gravity of its own) and the character lets go; letting go of Interact or leaving the ground lets go too. Holding an object, the character faces it and can't jump, dash, crouch or roll. Without Interact held, an object is just an obstacle. The Pushing & Pulling section of the BasicMovementMode asset tunes the speed and reach.

## Turning and Leaning
The character turns at once where it's steered, so it's always responsive, but its model (on the model parent, separate from the character's root for this) turns after it, naturally. It eases into and out of each turn (a damped spring on its yaw, rather than snapping most of the way round at the start), no faster than 900°/s standing still, falling to 420°/s at a run (8 m/s and up), so a sprinting character sweeps round a turn instead of spinning on the spot. It leans into the curve the character actually runs, by half the lean that would balance it: the angle whose tangent is how hard its path turns it (its velocity projected onto the way the model faces, times how fast its path curves) over gravity, so a sharp turn the model is still coming round to leans little. It leans forward as it speeds up and back as it stops, by a quarter of the balancing lean. Either way, it leans no further than its actual speed allows (1.5° per m/s, up to 15°), so walking leans about half as much as sprinting, even round a sharp corner (6.5° walking a right angle); at a steady speed or standing still it's upright. As the model turns about its feet, foot placement locks each planted foot where it was put down (see IK), so the feet don't slide round with it. Facings that abilities set (a dash, roll, slide, wall run, wall slide, ledge, or holding an object) are turned to almost at once, without leaning, and spinning ground turns the model with it. Only the model turns and leans: the character's collision and the camera, which follow the root, don't. The AnimationController's Smooth Rotation and Leaning settings tune it.

## Collision
MovementController resolves the character's movement against the world itself, with capsule sweeps rather than the physics solver. Each physics step it:
- pushes the character out of anything that moved into them since the last step, so moving walls and platforms push the character instead of passing through
- slides along walls and walkable slopes, and steps up stairs and low ledges, anything up to the step height, at any pace (a step-up carries the character far enough onto the step that it doesn't perch on the edge and slide back off)
- slides the character off surfaces too steep to stand on, including ledge edges caught by the capsule's rounded bottom; but the edge of a step (one with walkable ground no further than the step height below the character's feet) is ground, so on stairs whose treads are shallower than the capsule is wide, as the level's are, the character walks and sprints down resting on the steps' edges instead of floating off the top step
- snaps to the ground when walking down slopes and stairs

Momentum that a collision blocks is lost: bumping a ceiling ends the jump, and momentum into a wall doesn't build up and fling the character once they move clear of it. Each step's MovementData records which sides were blocked (`collisions`, as Unity's CollisionFlags), and the next step can read them (`previousCollisions`).

## Character Size
The character can be resized at runtime with `ACCharacterController.SetScale(Vector3)`, or by editing the Scale field in the ACCharacterController's inspector while playing. x is width, y height and z depth, relative to the character as authored; any mix of positive values works, including non-uniform and fractional scales. Negative, zero and non-finite scales are refused with an error (the inspector field reverts), and values under 0.05 are raised to it. Only the model parent is scaled, never the character's own transform; a scaled root (by itself or a parent) is reported as an error, since it would break collision. Everything else follows:
- **Collision:** the capsule's height scales with y and its radius with the larger of x and z (capped at half the height, so a wide, flat character doesn't get a collider taller than itself). Step height, ground snapping, the grounded tolerance, the ground check and the skin width (the small gap kept from surfaces) scale with it, so a tiny character obeys the same collision rules as a full-size one.
- **Room to grow:** a change of size takes a moment (Scale Change Duration). Growing pauses while there isn't room, under a low ceiling say, and carries on once there is; shrinking always happens.
- **Movement:** by default it scales in proportion: speeds, accelerations and gravity scale with height, and timings stay the same, so the character moves exactly like a scaled copy of itself from 0.1 to 100 times its size. It crosses its own height in the same time, and a jump lasts as long and goes as high for its size, so a small character doesn't look zippy and a big one doesn't look floaty. A test runs it over a course of ramps, a step, a jump and a dash at sizes from 0.1 to 100 and checks every physics step lands it in the same place for its size as at full size. The MovementController's Size Scaling setting can instead scale it naturally, as real animals do (speeds with the square root of height, gravity unchanged, so smaller characters look quicker for their size), or keep speeds unchanged.
- **Mass** scales with volume.
- **Animation:** the Animator gets speeds divided by the model's stride scale, so planted feet stay put at any size or stretch. Vertical speed is divided by how speeds scale, so the rising and falling poses fall at the same points of a jump. Foot placement's step limits scale too, and IK places hands and feet right when the model is stretched on one axis. Unity's humanoid IK gets stretched models wrong in two ways, and CharacterIK corrects both. It reports foot goals as if the model were scaled evenly by its width, so a taller-stretched model's feet were placed below the floor, and a shorter one's above it. And it solves each limb from where the limb starts (the hip joint, or the collarbone), moving the hand or foot further from there across and along the model than asked, by the model's width and depth scale over its height scale: a goal 5 cm in front of a hip put the foot 10 cm in front on a model twice as deep, and a hand on a model twice as wide missed by 44 cm. Feet now land within a millimetre of their goals and hands within one centimetre, on every model and stretch tested.
- **Feet on the ground:** every model's soles stay on the floor at any size, including stretched on one axis only. Prepare Character Models measures how far each model's soles stand from its origin and stores it as its source's Ground Offset, and straightens rotated nodes above the skeleton, which used to distort stretched models (see below).
- **The model's measurements**, the eye point and the placeholder all follow, and models loaded while the character is resized are measured at its size.
- **The camera:** first person follows the eye point. Third person's pivot height, shoulder offset and distance, and the head bob, scale with the camera rig's Target Size Reference, which a spawned character hands the camera (its model parent), through its spawner's camera event (see Spawning the Character). Speed effects (the speed-based field of view and head bob strength) judge the target's speed relative to its size, dividing it by its height, so a character at its own top speed gets the same effect at any size. Every other distance and speed the camera uses scales with the target too: follow lag, landing dip and its speed threshold, collision probe and minimum distance, hide distance, shake, teleport distance and the clip planes (near always, far only for a bigger target, so a small one still sees the world).

## Moving Ground
The character rides along with whatever it stands on. Each physics step, before moving, the character is carried by however far its ground moved, and turned with it if it spins. It then moves relative to the ground as normal. Leaving moving ground, by jumping or walking off, keeps the ground's velocity, so the character doesn't stop dead in mid-air. These options can be toggled on the MovementController.

Moving platforms should be kinematic Rigidbodies moved in FixedUpdate with interpolation enabled; the MovingPlatform component does this. Tools > Character Showcase > Create Foot Placement Test Area adds a sliding platform, an elevator and a spinning platform to try it.

## Camera System
The camera lives in Assets/Scripts/CameraSystem, in its own assembly with no dependency on the character code. Add a CameraRig component to a camera, give it a target, and every feature can be tuned or toggled from its inspector.

Features: first person (at a fixed eye height, or following an eye anchor Transform such as a character's eye point) and over-the-shoulder third person (scaled with an optional target size reference, so resized targets are framed alike) with smooth blending between them, zoom (zooming all the way in switches to first person), shoulder swapping (automatic when a wall blocks the current side), aiming, camera collision, follow damping, target interpolation for physics-driven targets, speed-based FOV, head bob, landing dips, trauma-based camera shake, optional auto-recentering, hiding the character up close, and CameraZone volumes that override camera behaviour in an area. Beside a wall that blocks its shoulder even partly (running along one, say), the camera glides to the other shoulder and keeps its distance, rather than being squeezed in behind the target's head against the wall; it returns once the wall is gone. A wall alongside never pulls the camera in: each collision sweep stops a little short of what it hits, so the camera's backward sweep never starts pressed against a wall it's running parallel to (which used to snap the camera in as a wall run began).

## Character Model and Animations
The character is a mannequin generated by a Blender script (Tools/Mannequin), rigged as a Unity Humanoid. It walks, jogs, sprints and jumps with Quaternius's Universal Animation Libraries (see Quaternius animations, below), and stands, walks briskly, runs and dashes with procedurally keyframed animations of its own (Tools/Mannequin/mannequin_animations.py): a still, evenly balanced stance, the gaits between the libraries' walk and jog, and a dash. An Animator blends between them using the character's speed, vertical speed, grounded and dashing state, and a Jump trigger. Crouching, sliding, rolling, wall-running, hanging, climbing and pushing each have a state of their own, entered from any state while the character does it (a Bool parameter of the same name) and left for whichever of standing, crouching or the air matches the character afterwards. They play the libraries' crouch (still, and crouch-walking by speed), slide, roll, climb and push; the roll and the climb play through once over however long the roll or climb is tuned to take (the RollProgress and ClimbProgress parameters drive their time), the push plays at the speed the character pushes and backward when it pulls (GrabSpeed), and a wall run plays the library's wall run for the side the wall is on, at the run's speed (the sprint, without the libraries). Hanging and sliding down a wall are poses of the mannequin's own, checked against the Quaternius library's climbs (which have no hang or wall slide of their own): the hands grip as far apart as theirs do (0.75 to 1.4 m), at head height on a wall, and the feet rest on the wall in front of the hips. Hanging is a pose of the mannequin's own (its hands land within a fifth of the character's height of the ledge), as is a crouch for when the libraries aren't installed; without them, a slide shows the dash, a roll the tuck at the top of a jump, a climb the rise, and a push the walk. Foot placement fades out while sliding and rolling, as it does dashing.

The Locomotion blend tree plays, by speed, each loop at the speed its strides are made for, measured on the mannequin, so its planted feet stay put there:

| Speed (m/s) | Animation |
|---|---|
| 0 | the stance (the mannequin's) |
| 0.97 | Quaternius's walk |
| 2.17 | the mannequin's brisk walk, then sped up 1.25 times by 3.2 |
| 3.77 | the mannequin's run |
| 5.79 | Quaternius's jog (sprinting) |
| 8.77 | Quaternius's sprint, then sped up to 10 m/s (the momentum a dash leaves) |

Each loop is offset to land its left foot at the same moment (TouchDownPhase, measured on the mannequin), so blending between them never mixes one foot's stride with the other's.

Jumping plays Quaternius's jump start from its take-off (the Jump trigger, fired by the Jumped and AirJumped movement events), skipping the crouch before it, since the character has already left the ground; then their jump loop while in the air, and their landing as soon as the character is grounded and no longer rising. Moving on (faster than 0.5 m/s) cuts the landing short. The Animator is given a vertical speed of 0 while the character touches the ground, where its vertical movement only follows the ground: walking up stairs lifts it several metres a second each step, which used to keep a character that landed on a staircase on the run in its jump all the way up. Their clips are raised or lowered so their lowest foot stands where the mannequin's stance does (their root height offsets, set by Generate Mannequin), so every clip shares one floor; the landing's crouch still puts the soles within about 4 mm of it, as the libraries' proportions differ a little from the mannequin's.

Without the libraries in the project, Generate Mannequin uses the mannequin's own clips throughout: a walk, the brisk walk, run and sprint (a gait that keeps its planted feet from sliding: each foot's pivot moves straight back at the sprint's speed while down, reached by inverse kinematics), and rising, a tuck at the top and falling, blended by vertical speed.

### Replacing the model
The model and its animations are kept separate from the character's movement, so they can be replaced without touching the character:

- **CharacterModelSource** is a ScriptableObject that gives the character its model: the character's CharacterModelHost calls its `CreateModelAsync(context, cancellationToken)` at runtime, which creates the model under the character, sets it up and hands it back as a `CharacterModel` (the instance, its Animator, IK and translator). It also holds the settings that apply however a model is stored: the translator that animates it, optional Animator Controller and Avatar overrides, placement, and whether to add IK to humanoids. To give the character a new look, assign a different source to the Character prefab's CharacterModelHost (or a scene's instance of it). Its Height setting either keeps the model's own size (times Scale) or fits it to a given height, for the character at its authored size: the character's own size multiplies either, so a model fitted to 1.8 m on a character twice the size is 3.6 m tall. Previews and the Character Model window's heights are sized the same way.
- **DirectCharacterModelSource** (Create > Character Showcase > Character Model (Direct Reference)) is the source that references its model directly: a prefab or model file, instantiated with `Object.InstantiateAsync` so even a heavy model doesn't stall the game. Prepare Character Models makes one for every model it sets up.
- **Other sources** subclass CharacterModelSource. One that loads from Addressables or an asset bundle overrides `CreateModelAsync` to load and instantiate the model, then calls `SetUpModelAsync` to apply the shared settings, and overrides `ReleaseModel` to release the instance (`Addressables.ReleaseInstance`) rather than destroy it. `SetUpModelAsync` is virtual and awaitable too, for applying a character creator's choices. If the cancellation token fires (the character was destroyed or given another model first), a source cleans up whatever it has loaded and throws `OperationCanceledException`; awaiting `Awaitable.NextFrameAsync(cancellationToken)` and similar does that.
- **CharacterModelHost** owns the character's model. It loads it at runtime, as the character's first step, so it never stalls the game, and the character moves normally while it loads. `await CharacterModelHost.LoadModelAsync(source)` swaps models at runtime, returning whether the new model loaded. New models are created hidden, under an inactive staging transform, and swapped in the same frame the old one goes, so two models are never seen at once; the old one goes back to the source that made it with `ReleaseModel`. While there's no model it shows a placeholder (a capsule the size of the collider), and if a model fails to load it loads its fallback source (the mannequin) instead. It keeps an Eye Point child at the model's eye height (from its eye bones, or partway up its head), which the camera's first-person view follows, and warns if a model is taller than the character's collider or much shorter. Its Collider Fit setting can instead resize the collider to each model as it's swapped in: Height makes it as tall as the model, keeping its proportions, and Height And Width also makes it as wide as the model's shoulders (times a margin for the arms). The character's size still multiplies the fitted collider, and like growing, fitting a bigger collider waits until there's room. Off by default, so the collider stays as tuned. AnimationController hears about new models through its `ModelLoaded` event and animates them; it no longer handles models itself. A model swapped in mid-move carries on where the old one was, rather than starting over from standing. Just before the swap, `ModelReplacing` lets the old model's animation driver record its state (`Retire`), and the new model's driver takes it up (`ContinueFrom`). For an Animator, that's its parameters, and each layer's weight, state, how far through it, and any transition it was part-way through, matched by name, so variants of one controller carry over. Foot placement carries over too, so planted feet stay planted rather than fading back in. A translator for another animation system can do the same by implementing those two methods; by default they do nothing.
- **CharacterAnimationState** is the layer between movement and animation: velocity (world and relative to facing), speeds, turn rate, grounded (with a short grace period for small step-offs), time in the air, moving, jumping and dashing. Animation code reads this rather than the movement system's data. Components on a model, like FootPlacementIK, find it through `ICharacterAnimationStateSource` in their parents, so they work on any character.
- **Movement events** are raised by `MovementController.MovementEventRaised` after each physics step: Jumped, AirJumped, Landed, LeftGround, DashStarted, DashEnded, HitCeiling and HitWall. Movement modes report the ones they cause (jumps and dashes), and the controller detects the rest. `AnimationController.MovementEventReceived` passes them on with the animation state, for effects and sound.
- **CharacterAnimationTranslator** (a ScriptableObject) turns the animation state and events into a particular model's animations. The included **AnimatorParameterTranslator** sets Animator parameters you name (speed, vertical, forward and sideways speed, turn speed, grounded, jumping, dashing, moving) and fires triggers on chosen movement events, skipping any the controller doesn't have. A different animation system can be supported by writing another translator.

In the editor, where the model isn't loaded, the CharacterModelHost draws it as a gizmo so the character can still be seen and placed.

### Character models
Character models live in subfolders of Assets/Models/CharacterModels: the generated mannequin, Quaternius's female mannequin (CC0, from the Universal Animation Library), and a custom model with three material variants. Any rigged humanoid model can be added the same way. Every model plays the mannequin's animations through Humanoid retargeting. Three tools under Tools > Character Showcase manage them:

1. **Generate Mannequin** runs Tools/Mannequin/generate_mannequin.py in Blender (asking where Blender is the first time) to build the mannequin's mesh, rig and animation clips. It then imports them as looping, in-place Humanoid clips, builds the Animator Controller every model shares (Assets/Animation/Mannequin.controller, rebuilt in place so the models' references to it never break) and its translator, prepares the mannequin like any other model, and makes it the Character prefab's model. If Blender isn't available, it can rebuild everything from the Mannequin.fbx already in the project. To change the mannequin or its animations, edit generate_mannequin.py or mannequin_animations.py and run this again.
2. **Prepare Character Models** imports and sets up every model file in Assets/Models/CharacterModels. It makes each a Humanoid, and straightens its arms and legs into a T-pose for the avatar's reference pose, so models rigged in an A-pose don't hold their arms up in every animation. It saves a model prefab next to each model ("[name] Model.prefab", with its Animator, CharacterIK, FootPlacementIK, LedgeHandIK and WallSlideIK) and a DirectCharacterModelSource ("[name].asset"). A folder can hold several model files, such as variants of one model; each is its own choice, and its prefab and source are always named after its file, so adding or removing other models never renames them. Models that aren't rigged, or whose bones Unity can't map to a Humanoid, are reported and skipped. Each saved prefab is read back from disk and checked for its avatar and the shared Animator Controller, so a model is only reported ready, and listed in the window, once it can actually animate. Models with lost units (a 180 m or 1.8 cm character) get their source scaled to 1.8 m. IK settings and edits to the model sources are kept. It runs by itself: when model files in the folder are added, changed, moved or deleted, or animation they play changes (an Animator Controller, a clip, or a model source naming a different controller), it prepares whichever are new or changed once the editor is idle (a new model is imported as a Humanoid from the start), and skips the rest; the menu item prepares them all regardless. Prepared assets found under other names (an older naming scheme, or a renamed file) are moved to the current names, keeping their references, and sources whose model file is gone are reported rather than deleted.
3. **Set Up Scene…** opens a window for choosing which character, model (any Prepare Character Models has set up, with previews and heights) and movement mode a scene spawns, and sets the open scene up to spawn it. See Spawning the Character. The choice is stored on the scene's Character Spawner, so the Character prefab itself, which the tests spawn, keeps the mannequin.

All three also run from the command line for automation: `-executeMethod CharacterShowcase.EditorTools.MannequinGenerator.GenerateFromCommandLine` (with `-blender <path>` or `-skipBlender`), `...CharacterModelImporter.PrepareAll`, and `...SceneGameSetup.SetUpFromCommandLine -scene Assets/Scenes/SampleScene.unity -model "base_basic_shaded"` (see Spawning the Character).

A Humanoid avatar's reference pose decides how every clip looks on that model, so Prepare Character Models builds it carefully. It starts from the skin's bind pose, the pose the mesh was modelled in, rather than the pose the skeleton is saved in, which can be an animation frame: Unity takes an FBX's default pose from its first take, and the mannequin's used to be the dash, so every mannequin clip was stored relative to a forward lean and other models played them leaning about 45 degrees back. (The mannequin now exports a one-frame rest-pose take first, which isn't imported as a clip.) It then straightens the spine up, the arms out and the legs down. With that, every model holds its torso at the mannequin's lean and keeps its planted feet in place to within a few percent of its speed, with no per-model tuning.

Two more steps keep every model's feet on the ground at any size. Blender's FBX export rotates the armature node -90 degrees about X to convert axes. The Animator poses that node as the avatar stores it and places the body through it, so when the character was stretched along one axis only, the rotation turned the stretch onto another axis: twice the height put the hips four times as high and lifted the feet off the ground. The reference pose is now saved with nodes above the skeleton unrotated (and everything under them where it was), as models without such a node have it. A node that's also unevenly scaled has its scale's axes swapped with the turn, which keeps everything under it exactly as it was for turns of whole quarter turns (any axis conversion); one turned any other way can't be straightened without shearing what's under it, so it's left and reported. (An uneven scale on its own doesn't distort a stretched model, so it isn't reported.) Then the model's soles are measured standing in its idle clip, with its feet where foot placement holds them, and saved as its source's Ground Offset, by which the model is lowered so its soles, not its origin, rest on the ground. Its soles are also measured with its feet where its animation puts them, as its Animated Ground Offset: on a model of other proportions than the clips' own, the two differ by up to a centimetre, and foot placement raises or lowers the body by the difference, in proportion to how far it's faded out (in the air, dashing, or easing in on landing), so the soles rest on the floor whether it's on or off. (While it's partly on, for the eighth of a second it fades, Unity's IK blends the legs' joint rotations, which dips the feet up to a few millimetres.) And the way the body faces as it stands is measured: humanoid animation keeps the facing a model was made with (though it centres the body on the model's origin, however far off it was made), so a model made facing away from +Z gets a Facing Correction, to the nearest quarter turn, that turns it to face forward. Every mesh counts, including ones carried by a bone rather than skinned, such as shoes. Being inside the scaled model parent, the offset scales with the character. Sources made by hand or in code, which haven't been measured, have Measure Ground On Load set, and measure their model the same way when it loads (StandingPose, in the runtime code, does the measuring both here and at import). A source measures its model once, for every copy: the measurement is kept until its model, Animator Controller or Avatar changes, the source is edited, `ForgetMeasurement()` is called, or Play mode starts again. A source whose models may each differ (a character creator's, say) returns null from `ModelTemplate`, so each model is measured as it loads. It's measured again whenever the model, its controller or the clips those play change, since it depends on them: each model's record (in Library/CharacterShowcase/PreparedModels.txt) holds a hash of its file and of its controller with everything the controller depends on.

### Motion capture clips
Assets/Animation/CMU holds 145 motion-capture clips from the CMU Graphics Lab Motion Capture Database (http://mocap.cs.cmu.edu), free to use, modify and redistribute but not to sell as animations (see LICENSE.txt there). The character no longer plays them (the Quaternius animations replaced its walk, run and jump), but they're kept for mechanics to come. They cover walking, running, jumping, stairs and steps, crouching, sneaking and crawling, sitting and getting up, climbing, carrying, pushing and pulling, picking things up, falls and idles, sorted into folders and named for what they show; CLIPS.md lists each with CMU's description. They're stored with Git LFS too.

They've been edited for a character controller, in Blender, by the scripts in Tools/Animation. remove_root_motion.py takes the hips' travel across the ground out of each clip, keeping their height (jumps still rise) and the sway within each step. tpose_rest.py makes the T-pose each clip starts with its skeleton's rest pose. Tools > Character Showcase > Prepare Motion Capture Clips then makes each a Humanoid animation with its own avatar (each performer had their own proportions), mapped by the CMU skeleton's bone names, starting after the T-pose frame, its height based on the feet (so its floor is the model's own), and facing the way the body does, so every clip faces the character's forward whichever way it was captured. MotionCaptureLocomotion can cut loops from them: it searches a take's steady middle for the two frames a cycle apart whose poses, and how they're moving, match best. It also measures any loop's stride speed, the moment it lands its left foot, a jump's take-off and landing, and a clip's lowest foot, which Generate Mannequin uses on every clip.

Two things turned up fitting real motion capture to foot placement. On a model stretched along one axis, Unity reports foot goals scaled about the clip's root, so a clip whose height was kept as captured (the performer's floor, a little off the model's) had its feet planted centimetres too low on a stretched model; based on the feet, the clip's floor is the model's, and they're on the ground. And Unity's Foot IK option on a state, which would pin each model's feet to the clip's, has the same stretch problem, so it's left off.

### IK
CharacterIK (on each model, reachable via CharacterModelHost.IK) layers inverse kinematics over the animations. Hands and feet can be pulled to target positions and rotations, and elbows and knees bent toward hint positions. Each has its own 0-1 weight that fades smoothly in and out, blending with the animation underneath. Targets can be Transforms assigned in the inspector or positions set from code:

```csharp
ik.SetGoal(IKLimb.RightHand, doorHandle.position, doorHandle.rotation); // reach for a handle
ik.SetHint(IKLimb.LeftFoot, kneeTarget.position, 0.5f);                 // push the left knee outward
ik.ClearGoal(IKLimb.RightHand);                                         // fade back to the animation
```

FootPlacementIK uses the same system to plant the feet on slopes, stairs and bumps automatically: it raycasts under each animated foot, shifts the foot to the real ground while keeping the animation's foot lift, tilts planted feet to the slope, and lowers the pelvis so the lower foot can reach. It feeds a per-frame procedural channel underneath the manual goals, so targets set from code still take priority. To try it, run Tools > Character Showcase > Create Foot Placement Test Area to add stairs, ramps and bumps in front of the character.

On a model of other proportions than the clips were made for, Unity reports each hand's and foot's goal where the clip's own character had it, which can be a few centimetres from where this model has it (up to 7 cm for the CustomModel's hands). So a goal set to where the animation reports it moves the hand or foot there, and a goal at partial weight is blended from there. A goal at full weight lands where it's set.

To try manual targets in the editor, right-click the CharacterIK component and choose "Create Target Transforms For All Limbs", then enter Play mode, raise a weight, and drag the target around in the Scene view.


Foot placement also locks planted feet: as the animation plants a foot, where it was put down is remembered (relative to the ground under it, so it rides moving ground), and an IK goal blended over the animation holds it there until the animation lifts it, or the animation's foot gets more than 25 cm from it. The model turns after the character about its feet (see Turning and Leaning), which would otherwise drag planted feet round with it; turning on the spot, they slid 10 cm without locking, and don't with it. Foot Lock on FootPlacementIK sets how firmly (0 turns it off).

The Meridian Complex is a large graybox level for putting the character through its paces: about 400 × 400 m of terraces, stairs, ramps, bridges, towers, tunnels and measured test courses, in seven connected areas. It's built from blockout diagrams (the MeridianComplex-*.png sheets at the repository's root). The character isn't placed in the scene: the level marks where it can start (four spawn markers on the Concourse plaza), and Set Up Scene makes it spawn at one of them when you press Play (see Spawning the Character, below).

| Area | Where | What it's for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 01 Concourse | Centre | The hub: a plaza, a terrace ring at +8, upper terraces up to +20, and bridges out to the areas around it |
| 02 Kinetic Gardens | North-west | Open-air traversal: a beginner route of stairs, ramps and short gaps, and an advanced one of longer gaps, a beam and a ledge chain |
| 03 Ascension Spire | North | Vertical traversal: a central core at +20 and a summit tower climbed by a switchback stair to +56 |
| 04 Fabrication Hall | East | An industrial building: rooms on floors from -8 to +8, catwalks at +12, crates and shelves |
| 05 Suspension Yard | South-east | Aerial: tower platforms at +8, +16 and +24, suspended bridges, and jump and dash gaps |

Every area can be reached on foot, by stairs and ramps that keep to the character's step height and slope limit; the pieces that need a jump, a climb or a crouch are marked as such. Mechanics the character doesn't have yet (ladders, ropes, wall runs, climbable walls, pushable crates) have placeholder geometry and markers named "(future)", under Future Mechanics in each area. Each area's notes (in Assets/Scenes/MeridianComplex) say how its diagram was read and what was chosen where it was ambiguous.

The whole scene is generated: Tools > Character Showcase > Level > Build Meridian Complex rebuilds it (also `-executeMethod CharacterShowcase.EditorTools.Level.MeridianComplexBuilder.BuildFromCommandLine`), replacing it and its environment assets. From the menu it builds in the background, one area at a time, and bakes the lighting in the background too, with a window showing its progress (also under the editor's Background Tasks), so the editor keeps responding. The whole build takes a few seconds. The level's pieces aren't marked Contribute GI (it has no baked or realtime GI to use it), which used to make the bake pack lightmaps for every piece, taking about seven minutes. Until the environment is replaced, Cancel (or closing the window) leaves the level as it was. After that, closing the window lets the build finish on its own, and cancelling the bake saves the level without its baked environment lighting. Rebuilding replaces the scene, so change the builders (Assets/Editor/Level, one per area) rather than the scene. Collision is all box colliders: the angled solids (octagonal platforms, terraces cut at 45°) are filled exactly by several boxes each, rather than with mesh colliders. Each flight of stairs is one object: a single mesh of the whole flight (treads with an orange nosing along each front edge, risers, a stair-shaped face down each side, no hidden or overlapping faces) with a box collider for each step. Guardrails are graphite posts every 2 m under an orange top rail, with a mid rail and an orange fascia band along the edge they stand on, one mesh per run; each run's collider is still one solid box the rail's full height, so nothing slips between the rails. Underground rooms have a graphite skirting along their walls (to see only: it has no collider).

The pieces carry the style reference's architectural detail and structure. Long walls have concrete pilasters every 8 m, and every opening a graphite frame with an orange lip (jambs, a header and a sill where the opening has wall above or below it). Platforms without guardrails have a graphite coping round their top edge, so their edges (and the ledges to catch) read from a distance. Free-standing and tower columns are chamfered, with an orange strip up each face. Walkways and bridges stand on steel I-beam columns, suspended bridges hang on a truss under each side, ramps rest on I-beam supports, and the Fabrication Hall's north catwalk stands on braced frames on the floors below it, with brackets where it goes through the walls. All of the detail is to see only: columns keep the box collider of their footprint, and pilasters, frames, copings and brackets have none. Trusses and braced frames, which can be walked into, each have one box collider round the whole of them, so nothing slips between their members.

Lighting and utilities follow the style reference too. Underground, each room has a ceiling panel at each of its lights and each corridor a strip light down its ceiling, with a steel and an orange pipe run along every ceiling on hangers, and utility stations (a cabinet, a conduit and a vent) on the walls of the Service Network's rooms. Every doorway with wall above it has a light over it; the Concourse plaza has bollards round its edge; the larger Suspension Yard towers each have a floodlight mast; and every bridge between areas has a cyan accent post at each corner, so the ways between areas read from a distance. The Fabrication Hall's rooms have utility stations too. The fixtures in daylight glow (with the palette's two glowing materials, Light and Cyan) but cast no light unless you turn that on in the generation settings, since in the midday sun they'd add real-time lights for little; the level has the same 32 real-time lights it had. Bollards, floodlight masts and the stations' cabinets stand where they can be walked into and have box colliders; the rest is to see only.

Markings and wayfinding finish it: hazard stripes (orange and graphite, raised a centimetre like thick paint) along the edges you jump from at every marked gap; corner brackets and painted values (0.30 m, 2.0 m, 40°) round the Calibration Annex's test lanes and the playground's; a sign gantry over the start of every bridge between areas, naming where it goes; and each area's number and name ("01 Concourse" to "07 Calibration Annex") on a sign at its entrance. The lettering is TextMeshPro; its essential resources are in Assets/TextMesh Pro. Sign panels and their posts have box colliders; markings and lettering are to see only.

Polish after the concept art and the icons and decals sheet: orange banners with the Meridian mark ("///") on the Concourse's gate towers, the Ascension Spire's summit tower, the Suspension Yard's tall columns and beside the Fabrication Hall's entrance; orange bands up the summit tower; the Concourse plaza's tree on the dais, orange and white guide rings round it, orange lanes out to the broad stairs, and planters with trees; orange copings on the Kinetic Gardens' platforms; lawns with grass tufts and the odd shrub along their edges over the gardens' open ground and every platform top there, and over the Suspension Yard's ground (each lawn keeps back from platform edges and leaves paving round anything standing on the floor, so stairs, columns and routes stay paved); low-poly trees scattered over the gardens' and the Suspension Yard's open ground, only where nothing's within a few metres, so they never crowd a route; an orange overhead crane over the Fabrication Hall's workshop; a measuring ruler beside the Calibration Annex's jump lanes; a bracketed pad with cyan chevrons under each spawn point; and, under every marker for a mechanic to come, a cyan bracketed square with its name (LADDER, ROPE SWING, PUSHABLE CRATES). Pads, marks, lawns and trees find the floor under them once the whole level's built (the build's "finishing touches" step). Trees, planters and the crane have box colliders; the rest is to see only. The trees are seeded, so every build grows the same ones.

**Culling.** Cameras skip what's out of view (each railing run, stair, lawn chunk and so on is its own object) and, with each level's baked occlusion culling data, what's hidden behind walls, terraces and the ground. Building a level bakes it after the lighting (a few seconds; Assets/Scenes/[Level]/[Level]/OcclusionCullingData.asset), with only the solid pieces (blocks, solids, stairs, columns) as occluders. Grass tufts cast no shadows and aren't drawn beyond about 70 m (the lawn under them still is). The game camera needs Occlusion Culling ticked, as the demo's Main Camera has. To see the culling at work, open Window > Rendering > Occlusion Culling, select a camera, and use the Visualization tab.

**Generation settings.** The numbers the shared pieces are made with are in one asset, Assets/Editor/Level/GrayboxSettings.asset (Tools > Character Showcase > Level > Generation Settings selects it): stair profile and nosings; deck thickness, bridge length, walkway columns and trusses; guardrail height, posts and rails; wall pilasters, opening frames and skirtings; platform copings; column chamfers and accent strips; I-beams, trusses, braced frames and brackets; light fixtures (ceiling panels, strip lights, door lights, bollards, floodlights, accent posts); the underground lights; pipes, utility stations and vents; markings (hazard stripes, corner brackets, floor labels) and signs (gantries, area signs, lettering); dressing (banners, guide lines, spawn pads, future-mechanic marks, rulers, tower bands); foliage (tree and shrub sizes, planters, how far apart and how much room the scattered trees need, lawn cells and margins, grass tufts' density, height, draw distance and shadows, and the seed); culling (whether to bake occlusion, and its smallest occluder, smallest hole and backface threshold); and the sun and haze. Change a value and rebuild a level to see it (the Level Playground builds in a couple of seconds). It's made with its defaults the first time a level's built, if it's missing. Where the areas put things (their layouts, a stair's width) stays in each area's builder, and how the surfaces look in the materials themselves.

The levels share a palette of six materials, after the style reference's colours (graphite, concrete, steel grey and an orange accent), all made with the noise-built concrete Shader Graph (Assets/Materials/NoiseConcrete.shadergraph: world-space noise, no textures, with recessed panel seams, metallic and emission settings): Concrete (decks, floors, steps), Panel (walls and large masses, in 2 m panels with recessed seams, each panel a shade lighter or darker), Ground (darker, in 4 m paving slabs), Graphite (posts, rails, trims, machinery), Steel (columns, supports, catwalks, crates) and Accent (orange: top rails, nosings, fascias). Each piece chooses its own surfaces; builders pick one for a box or solid where the default, concrete, isn't right (`Graybox.Surface`). The materials (Assets/Materials/Level) are made the first time a level's built and then kept, so they can be tuned by hand; delete one to have the next build make it afresh.

**The Level Playground** (Assets/Scenes/LevelPlayground) is a small test scene with one of each piece the complex is built from, made by the same builders' pieces in the same material and lighting: an octagonal dais and a turned column at the spawn point; a terrace cut at 45° up a 1:4 ramp, with guardrails; a stair to a walkway on columns, two octagonal towers joined by a suspended walkway, a 6 m jump gap and a diagonal bridge out to a connection marker; a walled building with a doorway, a window, a crate and a shelf; an underground stairwell, passage, lit room and ventilation shaft at -6; one of each of the Calibration Annex's tests (steps either side of the step limit, a ledge, a 40° slope, a 1.5 m tunnel, a narrow beam beside a wall-run wall); one of each mechanic (a lift, a slide gate, a timed 20 m run and a crate to push; see below); and placeholders for mechanics to come. It builds in a couple of seconds (Tools > Character Showcase > Level > Build Level Playground, or `-executeMethod CharacterShowcase.EditorTools.Level.LevelPlaygroundBuilder.BuildFromCommandLine`), so changes to the level's pieces can be tried there before rebuilding the whole complex. Like the complex, rebuilding it removes its game setup: run Set Up Scene again afterwards.

It's lit as a sunny midday for URP: a high sun with soft shadows, a procedural sky that lights the ambient and reflections, light haze, and a global post-processing volume (ACES tonemapping, light bloom, a little contrast). The PC render pipeline asset's shadow distance is 150 m, up from 50, so the terraces' shadows reach across the hub.

## Test Level: The Proving Ground
The Proving Ground (Assets/Scenes/ProvingGround) is a level for trying out the movement: one circuit through six zones from a start hall, each pushing one family of moves, its gaps, runs and ledges set against what the character can do with the movement mode as it's tuned now (measured with the real movement code: a sprint jump crosses 6.8 m, 9.7 m with an air dash; a double jump 11.8 m, 14.5 m with an air dash; a ledge is caught up to 4.8 m up from a jump and 6.5 m from a double; a slide goes 5.7 m and a roll 3.7 m; a wall run lasts 11 m). Retuning the movement may call for the layout to be revised (its numbers are at the top of `ProvingGroundBuilder`). Every challenge has a way back up from a fall.
- **P0 Start Hall**: an octagonal plaza with four spawn points, and the lap gate, timing each lap of the circuit.
- **P1 Sprint Line**: 130 m east. The north lane is a 100 m speed trap (timed between two gates, with its time and speed on the finish gate, and a ruler marked every 10 m); the south lane has two slide gates 1.2 m clear and kerbs either side of the step height (0.40 m to hop, 0.30 m to run up).
- **P2 Gap Run**: a ramp up to platforms at +4 heading north over a park, the gaps between them graded to the jumps: 4, 6, 8.5, 11 and 13.5 m (a sprint jump, a sprint jump near its 6.8 m limit, a sprint jump and air dash, a double jump, a double jump and air dash), each painted with its width, with stairs back up from below.
- **P3 Wall-Run Canyon**: a canyon 4 m wide between walls to +14, over a pit at -4, from a run-up at +8 west to two landings at +4 and an exit: runs of 10 + 10 m (with a wall jump between) and 12 m (more than one run: it has to end in a jump).
- **P4 Ledge Tower**: a suspended walkway from the canyon to a deck at +4, and ledges up to a summit at +24, 4 to 6 m apart (a jump or a double jump to catch each, as painted on each); a chimney 2.5 m wide to climb by wall jumps; and a stair back down.
- **P5 Low Works**: an enclosed, lit hall with a crawl tunnel 1.2 m clear for 36 m, slide gates, a roll lane under hoods 3.5 m deep, a ledge at +7 out of reach without a crate to stand on and a double jump, and two crates to push (one, in an alcove, has to be pulled out).
- **P6 Moving Ground**: a pit at -6 crossed on shuttles, a turntable and islands, with a lift up from its floor and stairs out at both ends.

Orange guide lines on the ground lead from each zone to the next. Build it with Tools > Character Showcase > Level > Build Proving Ground (or `-executeMethod CharacterShowcase.EditorTools.Level.ProvingGroundBuilder.BuildFromCommandLine`), then Set Up Scene: as with the other levels, its moving platforms, crates and timing gates only work once the scene is set up.

**Level mechanics.** The level builders can't reference the game's code, so a mechanic is built as a fixture: its geometry, named for what it is, with its settings after the name. Set Up Scene turns each into the real thing (`LevelFixtureSetup`):
- "Moving Platform (…) | travel=x,y,z | period=s | pause=s | spin=x,y,z | phase=f | smooth=0/1" gets a kinematic Rigidbody and a MovingPlatform with those settings;
- "Pushable Crate | pull=0/1 | speed=f" gets a Rigidbody and a PushableObject (Can Be Pulled, Speed Multiplier);
- "Timing Gate | course=name | role=start-finish/start/finish | distance=m" (a trigger) gets a TimingGate, and each course a CourseTimer under the Game Setup object, told by the spawner who's racing; a gate's display, "Timing Display | course=name", shows its course's time under way, the last and best, and (with a distance) the speed.

A CourseTimer times laps through one start-and-finish gate (a pass less than a second after a lap starts is ignored), or runs from a start gate to a finish gate, in game time. Only its racer counts once it has one; until then anything passing with a Rigidbody does. The fixtures can also be added and named by hand in any scene that's then set up.

## Level Specs
Each level can also be kept as data: a level spec (Assets/Levels/[scene].level.json), listing the pieces it's built from in order, with their settings. A spec can be exported from a level's builder, edited in the Level Editor, written by hand or by other tools as JSON, XML or CSV, checked against what the character can do, drawn as a schematic, and built into a scene like any level.

**The format.** A spec is JSON, one line per piece:

```
{"format": "character-showcase-level", "version": 1, "name": "Proving Ground", "scene": "Assets/Scenes/ProvingGround/ProvingGround.unity",
 "parts": [{"name": "P2 Gap Run", "pieces": [
   {"piece": "Group", "parent": "", "name": "P2 Gap Run"},
   {"piece": "Platform", "parent": "P2 Gap Run", "name": "Platform A (+4)", "x0": 136, "x1": 148, "z0": 23, "z1": 39, "y0": 0, "top": 4, "coping": "Accent"},
   ...]}]}
```

Each piece is one of the builders' pieces (`Graybox`'s methods marked `[Piece]`), its settings named as its parameters; settings left out take their defaults. Points are `[x, z]` (or `[x, y, z]`), areas `[xMin, zMin, xMax, zMax]`, surfaces by name, and a parent is a path under the level's root (`""` the root, `"P2 Gap Run/Stairs"`, `"G#2"` for the second group named G). `Placed` and `Turned` make groups set at a position or turned, whose contents are laid out in their own coordinates. A `"State"` entry changes the stair step size or the deck and rail dimensions for the pieces after it in its part. The whole format, every piece and its settings and defaults, is described by a JSON Schema, Tools/LevelSpec/level-spec.schema.json, written by Unity from the pieces themselves (Tools > Character Showcase > Level > Specs > Write Spec Schema, and every export).

**In Unity** (Tools > Character Showcase > Level > Specs):
- **Export Level Specs** records each level's builder as it builds and writes its spec, then builds the level again from the spec and checks it's the same, object for object (the Meridian Complex, Level Playground and Proving Ground all are). A spec that's been changed since it was exported (in the Level Editor, say) is only replaced if you say so.
- **Build Level from Spec…** builds a level from a spec as its builder would: the scene the spec names, with its environment and bakes, ready for Set Up Scene.
- From the command line: `-executeMethod CharacterShowcase.EditorTools.Level.LevelSpecTools.ExportFromCommandLine` (optionally `-level "Proving Ground"`, and `-force` to replace specs changed since they were exported; it exits with 1 if a spec doesn't build its level or was kept) and `...LevelSpecTools.BuildFromCommandLine -spec Assets/Levels/ProvingGround.level.json`.

**The Level Editor** (Tools > Character Showcase > Level > Level Editor) edits a spec and shows it as it changes. Open a spec (or start a new level), then Preview builds it in an unsaved scene, without bakes. The window shows the spec's parts and pieces in a list, the picked piece's settings, and a plan of the level drawn from the preview, shaded by height. Pick a piece in the list, on the plan or in the Scene view (each selects the others), and change its settings in the inspector, drag it on the plan, or move it with the Scene view's handle (both snap to the grid: the Snap setting in the toolbar). Each edit rebuilds just the piece it changed, with the finishing touches that look at what's round them (lawns, trees, spawn pads), so the preview stays as a full build would make it.

Adding and removing pieces:
- **Add** (above the list, or in the panel the editor shows in the Scene view's corner) lists every kind of piece by what it's for. Pick one, then click where it goes on the plan or in the Scene view: it's put there, standing on whatever's under the click, in the picked piece's group (or the picked group), after the picked piece. Enter puts it at the middle of the plan; Esc cancels. Each kind starts small, ready to be sized in the inspector.
- **Right-click** on the plan for Add Here (at the click), and Duplicate, Delete, Move Up/Down or Frame for the piece under it; on a piece in the list for the same; on a part's name to add a piece or part, or delete the part.
- **Duplicate** (Ctrl/Cmd+D) copies the picked piece, 2 m along; **Delete** (the Delete key) removes it, in the window or the Scene view, from the spec and the scene. The inspector has both buttons too.
- Pieces whose objects are deleted from the scene another way (the Hierarchy's Delete) are taken out of the spec as well, so the two agree (undo that with Edit > Undo).

Pieces can be reordered in their part (▲ ▼), and parts added, renamed, reordered and deleted. Edit > Undo and Redo step through the edits. Save writes the spec. Build Level builds the scene, with its environment and bakes; then Set Up Scene… makes it playable. Schematic opens the level's schematic with the movement checks (it needs Python 3). On the plan, right-drag or Alt-drag pans and the wheel zooms.

**Outside Unity**, the levelspec tool (Tools/LevelSpec, Python 3 with nothing else to install) reads specs written as JSON, XML or CSV, checks them, converts between them, and draws schematics:

```
python3 Tools/LevelSpec/levelspec validate my_level.xml          # against the schema: every problem, where it is
python3 Tools/LevelSpec/levelspec check my_level.xml             # against what the character can do
python3 Tools/LevelSpec/levelspec schematic my_level.xml plan.html   # or plan.svg
python3 Tools/LevelSpec/levelspec convert my_level.xml my_level.csv  # any format to any other
python3 Tools/LevelSpec/levelspec build my_level.xml --unity     # write Assets/Levels/[scene].level.json and its schematic, then have Unity build and set up the scene
python3 Tools/LevelSpec/levelspec pieces [Platform]              # the pieces, or one piece's settings
```

XML and CSV take settings as text: numbers, points as `136, 23`, lists with `;` between items (`18, 7.5; 7.5, 18`), or any value as JSON. In XML, each piece is an element named for it (`<Platform name="A" x0="0" x1="12" .../>`), and pieces inside a `<Group>` are put in it. In CSV, each row is a piece and each column a setting, with a `Level` row giving the level's name and scene. See Tools/LevelSpec/examples for a level written in each, and the notes in Tools/LevelSpec/levelspec/formats.py. Converting a spec to XML or CSV and back gives the same spec, byte for byte.

**Movement checks** hold a level to what the character can do, as tuned: Tools/LevelSpec/envelope.json, the limits measured with the movement code (revise them with the tuning). They report stairs with steps over the step height, ramps steeper than 1:4, low beams too low to crouch under, and, between the level's blocks and platforms, each gap at the same height and each climb to a neighbour, with the move it takes (a 6 m gap at +4: a sprint jump; 6 m up: a double jump to the ledge) or that it's out of reach. Walls (Panel blocks) aren't counted as somewhere to stand. The checks are reports, not errors: a pit too wide to jump may have stairs out of it.

**Schematics** are drawn from a spec: a plan of every piece where it is, shaded by height, with stairs and ramps, guardrails, mechanics (moving platforms with their travel, crates, timing gates, low beams), markings, lawns and trees, and the movement checks over it. As HTML (pan and zoom, layers to show and hide, each piece's settings on hover, the checks listed and found on the plan) or SVG. The plans are worked out from the pieces' settings (Tools/LevelSpec/levelspec/geometry.py), so a new piece needs an outline there to be drawn as more than a mark.

## Spawning the Character
A scene doesn't need the character placed in it: a **CharacterSpawner** (Add Component > CharacterSpawner) spawns it. It takes a character prefab (and optionally a model and a movement mode asset to give the spawned character instead of the prefab's own), a list of spawn points (a world position for the character's feet and a rotation for the way it faces), and a Spawn On Start checkbox. With Spawn On Start ticked, it spawns the character when the scene starts, at one of its spawn points chosen at random; untick it to spawn from code with `Spawn()` (a random point) or `SpawnAt(index)`. Each call spawns another character. The spawn points are drawn in the Scene view as capsules, with an arrow for the way each one faces. The spawner tells whatever needs the new character about it through its On Character Spawned event, which can be wired up in the inspector, and the `CharacterSpawned` C# event. A camera gets the character from its On Camera Target Spawned event, with the character's eye point and size reference (its model parent, whose scale is its size), for the camera rig's `Follow(target, eyeAnchor, sizeReference)`: the camera snaps to it, looks the way it faces, as it does at the start, puts the first-person view at its eyes and frames it at its size. (`Follow(Transform)` takes just a target.) The spawner's Start runs before other scripts' Start, so a camera's Start already sees the spawned character.

**Tools > Character Showcase > Set Up Scene…** sets up the open scene to spawn a character. In its window, choose:
- the character prefab
- its model: the prefab's own, or any model Prepare Character Models has set up, shown with a preview and its height
- its movement mode asset

The choices start from the scene: its existing setup's, or else its placed character's (the prefab it's an instance of, its model and movement mode), or else the package's Character prefab as it is. Then press Set Up. The scene gets a Game Setup object with a Character Spawner that spawns the character on Start and hands it to a camera. The camera is the scene's own camera rig if it has one, or else a copy of the demo scene's Main Camera (so it's set up and tuned the same). The input manager is likewise the scene's own, or a copy of the demo's. Only the model and movement mode that differ from the prefab's are stored on the spawner, so later changes to the prefab carry through.

Where the character spawns, in order of preference:
1. The scene's existing setup's spawn points: setting up a scene again replaces its setup but keeps them.
2. The level's spawn markers (objects named "Spawn Point …", facing their forward) and where the scene's placed characters stand.
3. The ground below the middle of the Scene view, facing the way the view looks.

Move or add spawn points on the Character Spawner afterwards. Characters placed in the scene are replaced by spawn points where they stood, unless you untick that (they're then left alongside the spawned one). The scene's other cameras are turned off (their objects deactivated, not deleted), unless you untick that too. The change can be undone and isn't saved for you. To change the demo scene's model, open it and set it up with the model you want: its placed character, if it still has one, becomes a spawn point. The window's buttons set up the model files (Prepare Character Models) and the animation files (the Quaternius libraries and motion-capture clips), and Refresh lists newly prepared models.

The Meridian Complex's builder places its spawn markers, so after rebuilding the level (which replaces its scene, setup included), open it and press Set Up to spawn there again. From the command line, with Unity closed, `-executeMethod CharacterShowcase.EditorTools.SceneGameSetup.SetUpFromCommandLine -scene <scene path>` sets a scene up and saves it, optionally with `-prefab <path>`, `-model <a prepared model's name or source path>` and `-movementMode <asset path>`. Run after building the level, it sets that up too.

## Automated Tests
The tests are written to hold for any team's setup, not just this project's: they read what they need from the character (its capsule, step height, slope limit, walking and dashing speeds, jump count) and from whatever model, animations and camera settings it has, and size their test worlds from those. Play mode tests run on the package's Character prefab unless a CharacterTestSettings asset (Create > Character Showcase > Tests > Character Test Settings) names another: put one anywhere in the project to run the whole suite on your own character. A test of something the character doesn't have (an air jump on a character with one jump, foot placement on a model that isn't a humanoid) is skipped, with the reason, rather than failed. They've been run on a character tuned differently (a smaller capsule, a lower step height and slope limit, half the walking speed, a slower dash and a single jump).

Tests of the package's own content (the mannequin and other prepared models, the motion-capture clips, the shared Animator Controller) are in the SampleContent category, and skipped where that content isn't there. A team using its own models and animations can leave them out: in the Test Runner, or with `-testCategory "!SampleContent"` on the command line.

Play mode tests in Assets/Tests/PlayMode spawn the character with real physics and drive its MovementController with scripted inputs. Each test's character moves with its own copy of its movement mode asset, so a test can change settings without changing the asset, and the asset can be retuned freely: the tests read the values they expect from the copy. Together with the camera tests, they cover:
- settling on the ground without hovering, sinking or drifting
- falling within gravity's limits, including walking off a ledge after standing still
- stopping at walls, jumping and landing
- climbing stairs and standing on a ramp without sliding (each a little over half the character's step height and slope limit)
- climbing any step up to the step height at walking and running pace, and not climbing taller ones
- riding sliding, rising and spinning platforms, and keeping a platform's momentum when jumping off
- coyote time easing gravity in after leaving the ground, and jumps not being refunded by it
- sprinting at the run speed and walking again once Sprint is let go, Sprint alone not moving the character, and a sprint's speed carried through a jump without stacking
- dashing: distance and direction, ignoring gravity, momentum afterwards, cooldown and input buffer, one dash per press, and air dash limits
- jump and dash cancelling each other
- crouching: lowering the collider to the crouch height (at twice the size too) and moving at the crouch speed, standing again on a second press, staying crouched under a low ceiling and standing by itself once out from under it, hold to crouch, one crouch per held press, jumping from a crouch (and not under a low ceiling), and its events
- sliding: crouching while sprinting slides, starting at least at the slide start speed, only slowing, low, ending crouched within its longest time; crouching while walking just crouching; sliding under a bar too low to stand under; jumping out keeping its speed; stopping at a wall; a second press standing up; and a slide length of 0 turning it off
- rolling: in the input direction for its duration, low, fast, facing it and standing afterwards; straight ahead without input and not steerable; one roll per press, a buffered press waiting out the cooldown; not in the air; ending crouched under a low ceiling; twice as far at twice the size; and handing straight over to walking without a burst of speed
- wall-running: running along a wall in mid-air, falling much slower than falling freely, staying against it and facing along it; ending after its longest time, past the end of the wall, and not starting again on the same wall; wall jumps away and up, giving back jumps, and chaining into a run on a facing wall; no run without steering, heading into the wall, close to the ground, or with a length of 0
- wall sliding: sliding slowly down a wall fallen into head-on, against it and facing it; a fast fall braked to the slide speed; jumping off upward and away, gaining height and giving back jumps; only so many jumps before landing; not while rising, close to the ground, or glancing along the wall; letting go by steering away or with Crouch; ending on landing and after its longest time; and a longest time of 0 turning it off
- ledges: grabbing a ledge falling in front of it and hanging still below it; climbing up onto it, or crouched with only room to crouch; letting go with Crouch or steering away; not grabbing ledges out of reach, or while rising fast; hanging from a moving ledge; climbing from a ledge onto stairs rising from it; letting go when the ledge's object is deactivated; jumping up to a ledge, grabbing and climbing it with its events raised once each; and a climb duration of 0 turning ledges off
- timed courses: sprinting from a start gate to a finish gate timed at the course's length over the run speed (with its speed shown), a lap gate starting a lap on the first pass and ending it on the next but not straight away, and only the racer counting
- pushing and pulling: pushing and pulling at the push speed with the character keeping hold, slower for heavier objects, push-only objects staying put, stopping at walls without going into the object, letting go, objects falling off edges, objects just being obstacles without Interact, and not jumping while holding one
- sample content: crouching, sliding, rolling (its animation following the roll, and blending into walking before the roll ends), wall-running (the wall run for the side the wall is on: feet toward the wall, leaning away), sliding down a wall (hands flat on it at head height, spread as wide as the library's climbs hold them, the body against it), hanging, climbing (staying in the climb when crouched) and pushing each playing their Animator state; landing on a staircase on the run (walking or sprinting up it) landing, not staying in the air; a hanging model's wrists on the ledge's top just back from its edge, either side of the character and as far apart as the library's climbs grip, fingers over the edge, its body against the wall; and a crouched model fitting its lowered collider
- movement at the edges of its settings, each set on the test's own copy of the movement mode: no walking or running speed (standing still without breaking), a run speed no faster than walking (Sprint walks), no jumps and many (exactly that many), no gravity (a jump drifting up without breaking) and fifty times the gravity with no fall speed limit (landing without going through the floor), walking at 200 m/s and dashing at 500 m/s into a wall a tenth of a metre thick (stopping at it), a dash with no length (no dash), no speed (holding still, then ending), an empty speed curve, or no cooldown (still one dash per press), no air dashes (ground dashes only), no jump input buffer (only a fresh press jumps) and no coyote time (a jump just after walking off is an air jump)
- keeping speed when walking off edges, walking smoothly down stairs (and walking and sprinting down a forty-step flight of steps nearly the step height, shallower than the character is wide, from different starting points and headings, without leaving them or speeding up), sliding off the edge of a drop taller than a step, air steering that doesn't stack on carried momentum, and the jump input buffer
- collisions: falling away from a ceiling the character jumps into, losing momentum into a wall, sliding off slopes too steep for it, and being pushed by moving walls
- movement events: each raised once at the right moment, and passed on by the AnimationController
- moving like a scaled copy of itself at any size from 0.1 to 100: over a course laid out for the character (ramps up and down, a step, a jump, a dash, a low ceiling and a step too tall for it), every physics step puts it in the same place for its size as at full size, on the ground at the same moments, with the same jump airtime and height for its size
- character size: scaling only the model parent and resizing collision and mass to match, size changes taking their time, waiting for room to grow under a low ceiling, non-uniform scales still moving and colliding, natural speed scaling, the model's measurements and placeholder following, and the inspector field
- refusing negative, zero and non-finite scales, raising tiny ones to the minimum, and reporting a scaled root transform
- the character's model standing with its soles on the floor (within 2 mm, scaled) at 0.1, 1 and 10 times its size and stretched on one axis, with foot placement on, off or fading; and an unmeasured source measuring its model on load
- its planted feet sliding no more at other sizes, or stretched, than at its own size
- IK on stretched models: moving a hand or foot goal moving the hand or foot as far, and a knee hint on its own moving the foot no more than unstretched
- models made facing another way, or off their origin, facing forward and standing on the spot; and a model with an unevenly scaled armature staying in proportion when stretched
- model hosting: loading at runtime and being set up, swapping models (including one without an Animator), new models staying hidden until swapped in, the placeholder, falling back when a model fails, cancelling loads when the character is destroyed or given another model, sources releasing their models, a model swapped in mid-run or mid-transition carrying on where the old one was, measuring on load once per model, and the editor preview
- fitting a model to a height (in proportion to the character's size, the eyes following), fitting the collider to the model by height or shoulder width, and waiting for room to grow
- the Animator parameter translator
- the camera rig, which follows a plain Transform, independent of the character code, with expectations taken from its own settings:
  - third and first person placement, and mode blending and events
  - pitch limits and zoom limits
  - collision with walls (only as far in as needed, ignoring walls clear of it and the target's own colliders), at 0.1, 1 and 10 times the target's size
  - moving to the other shoulder, at full distance, beside a wall partly blocking its own, returning once the wall is gone, and staying put beside a wall on the other side; and never pulling in as a target comes up against a wall and runs along it
  - smooth follow and teleport snapping
  - shake and ShakeAll
  - first person target hiding, including renderers added to the target later
  - first person eyes following an eye anchor
  - framing and clip planes scaled with the target, at 0.1, 2, 10 and 100 times its size
  - speed FOV judging speed relative to the target's size, and settling once it stops
  - the landing dip, as deep as the landing settings make it, in proportion to the target's size, and recovering (landings are judged by the fastest fall since the target last rose, so a frame holding several physics steps, as in the editor, doesn't halve the impact)
  - CameraZone overrides
  - following a new target with `Follow`: snapping to it and looking the way it faces, and taking its eye anchor and size reference in place of the old target's
- foot locking: turning on the spot, planted feet staying where they were put down (none of the 10 cm they slide without it)
- the model turning after the character: the character turning at once and the model easing round after it without overshooting, turning slower at a run than standing (no faster than its moving limit), leaning into curves by the configured share of the balancing lean (either way), walking a curve leaning about half as much as sprinting it, walking a sharp corner leaning no more than walking speed allows, leaning forward speeding up and back stopping and upright otherwise, facing a roll at once without leaning, and turning with a spinning platform without lagging
- the character spawner: spawning at a spawn point and facing its way, at a chosen point or one chosen at random from all of them, on Start or only when asked, telling listeners through both events, treating an all-zero rotation as none, giving the spawned character the chosen model and movement mode (and keeping the prefab's without them), giving the camera its eye point and size reference, and refusing with an error when there's no prefab, no spawn points or no such point
- sample content: every prepared model's planted feet staying in place walking at the walk loop's speed, standing on the floor, arms hanging down when standing, and the torso leaning as the mannequin's does when standing, walking and running (checks that its avatar's reference pose was set up)

Edit mode tests in Assets/Tests/EditMode check Prepare Character Models and the motion-capture clips without entering Play mode, so a change to preparing (or a model added without preparing it) fails fast:
- every model file has a source
- straightening nodes above the skeleton keeping everything under them where it was, unevenly scaled ones included, and leaving what can't be straightened
- facing corrections rounding to the nearest quarter turn
- ground offsets counting meshes carried by bones, not just skinned ones
- the level editor: adding a piece where it's placed (in the picked piece's group, standing on it), deleting it as the Delete key does (the scene left as it was) and undoing that, duplicating a piece, and a piece whose objects are deleted from the scene taken out of the spec; moving a piece moving its positions and not its directions or sizes; a new piece of every kind being valid and building; the preview knowing which piece made each object; rebuilding one edited piece (a platform, a spawn marker, a turntable) leaving the level as building the edited spec afresh would; and an edit undone putting the spec and the preview back
- level specs: every level built again from its own spec (written as JSON and read back) building the same level, object for object; a hand-written spec building what it lists, with State entries and groups of the same name; mistakes (unknown pieces and settings, missing settings, wrong shapes, bad JSON, a missing parent) reported with where they are; numbers reading back as the same float; and the schema covering every piece
- level fixtures (Set Up Scene): settings read from a fixture's name, moving platforms and crates given what they need from them, and a timer for each course, its gates' roles and distance, and its display wired to it
- sample content: setting up a scene: replacing a placed character with a spawn point where it stood (or keeping it, when asked), spawning at the scene's spawn markers, keeping its spawn points and replacing its old setup when set up again, wiring the demo scene's camera (or the scene's own camera rig) to the spawner, copying the input manager once, and turning other cameras off rather than deleting them
- sample content: every prepared model's source is ready to use and plays the shared Animator Controller; its avatar reference pose is a T-pose (spine up, arms out, legs down, within 2 degrees) with nothing rotated above its skeleton; its stored Ground Offset, Animated Ground Offset and Facing Correction match a fresh measurement; what it was prepared against changes when its controller changes; and previews and listed heights are sized like the loaded model
- sample content: the shared Animator Controller's Locomotion blend tree lines up every loop to land its left foot at the same moment, and each loop shown at its own strides' speed (the walks, the jog, and the sprint sped up to the top) keeps its feet in step with the ground there
- sample content: every motion-capture clip is a Humanoid animation with a valid avatar, mapped by the CMU bone names and starting after its T-pose frame, and played on the mannequin, every clip that moves about on its feet stays on the spot (its hips within 32 cm, 9 cm typically) and faces forward (within 6 degrees); each check lists every clip that fails it

The levelspec tool's tests run with `python3 -m unittest discover -s Tools/LevelSpec/tests`: the exported specs valid, every format converting to every other and back unchanged, XML and CSV written by hand reading as meant, mistakes reported where they are, groups' placement carried to their contents, the Proving Ground's gaps and ledges graded as built, and schematics drawn.

Run them from Window > General > Test Runner (the EditMode and PlayMode tabs), or from the command line with Unity closed:

```
/Applications/Unity/Hub/Editor/<version>/Unity.app/Contents/MacOS/Unity -batchmode -projectPath . -runTests -testPlatform EditMode -testResults EditModeResults.xml
/Applications/Unity/Hub/Editor/<version>/Unity.app/Contents/MacOS/Unity -batchmode -projectPath . -runTests -testPlatform PlayMode -testResults PlayModeResults.xml
```

CI (.github/workflows/tests.yml) runs both.

The character scripts are in the CharacterShowcase assembly (Assets/Scripts), and editor tools are in CharacterShowcase.Editor (Assets/Editor), so the tests can reference them. The level builders are in their own assembly, CharacterShowcase.Level.Editor (Assets/Editor/Level), which doesn't reference the character or camera code.
